**Note:** The de facto regime classification is based on IMF AREAER sources up to 2023. For 2024–2025, no final IMF de facto classification was used; these entries are inferred from NBU communications and policy announcements. They are therefore not strictly comparable to the IMF-based classifications reported for earlier years.

**Note on inflation targeting:** The NBU announced the transition in August 2015, formally adopted the framework in December 2016, and policy behaviour became progressively consistent with IT during 2016–2017. Target path: 12% ±3pp (end-2016) → 8% ±2pp (end-2017) → 6% ±2pp (end-2018) → 5% ±1pp (from end-2019 onward).

**Periods of significant capital controls:** October–December 2008; 2014–2015; February 2022 onward under martial law (NBU Resolution No. 18).

### When did Ukraine have monetary sovereignty, and what does this imply for euro adoption?

Monetary sovereignty in Ukraine varied across exchange rate regimes. It was highest under floating exchange rates and inflation targeting (post-2015, especially 2017–2021), when the National Bank of Ukraine could set interest rates independently and use the exchange rate as a shock absorber. By contrast, sovereignty was limited under fixed or tightly managed exchange rate regimes (2002–2007, 2010–2013, and again in 2022), where the exchange rate acted as the nominal anchor and constrained monetary policy. Euro Area membership would imply a permanent loss of monetary sovereignty, eliminating the exchange rate as an adjustment tool, as emphasized by Mundell (1961). Consequently, the impact of euro adoption is time-varying: it is limited during peg periods but larger during episodes of genuine monetary autonomy.
## Part B — Counterfactual: Method and Results

### Method

A bivariate SVAR is estimated separately for Ukraine and the Euro Area using output growth and inflation, with Blanchard–Quah long-run identification. The counterfactual keeps Ukrainian supply shocks unchanged and replaces Ukrainian demand shocks with euro area demand shocks. A time-varying regime weight delta(t), derived from the Part A chronology, then scales the intensity of the counterfactual across periods.

```
pi_cf_bq[t] = ua_supply_component[t] + ea_demand_component[t]
pi_final[t] = delta[t] * pi_cf_bq[t] + (1 - delta[t]) * pi_actual[t]
```

### What does the counterfactual imply about the cost or benefit of monetary sovereignty for Ukraine?

All variables are stationary, ensuring a valid VAR representation, and the counterfactual combines Ukrainian supply shocks with euro area demand shocks. During peg periods (2005–2007, 2010–2013), the counterfactual stays close to observed inflation, consistent with Ukraine’s limited effective monetary autonomy under exchange-rate targeting. By contrast, deviations are larger in non-peg periods, especially during major macroeconomic shocks.
In 2008–2009 and 2014–2015, the counterfactual implies much lower inflation than observed. This suggests that monetary sovereignty gave Ukraine an important adjustment margin during these crises, mainly through exchange-rate depreciation, but at the cost of much higher inflation through pass-through to domestic prices. Under euro area membership, adjustment would instead have occurred through domestic prices and output (internal devaluation), implying lower inflation but more constrained macroeconomic adjustment, in line with De Grauwe (2012).
After the adoption of inflation targeting in 2016, the gap narrows, suggesting that improved policy credibility reduced the marginal benefit of importing the ECB’s nominal anchor. The counterfactual does not imply lower inflation in all periods: in 2022–2023, inflation is higher under euro area membership, suggesting that monetary sovereignty may also have provided useful flexibility in the face of an extreme adverse shock. Overall, the results point to a standard OCA trade-off: monetary sovereignty gave Ukraine greater crisis-adjustment capacity, but often at the price of weaker nominal stability and higher inflation.
The magnitude of these effects should be interpreted with caution, as the Ukrainian output series is constructed from annual data interpolated at quarterly frequency, which may distort short-run dynamics.
